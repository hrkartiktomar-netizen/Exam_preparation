# An Analysis and Recommendations
Report on the Proposed IFSCA FinTech
Sandbox Framework: A Roadmap to
Global Leadership

## Executive Summary

The International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) has taken a commendable and
proactive step in proposing a dedicated and unified FinTech Sandbox Framework. This
initiative is a critical enabler for realizing the vision of the Gujarat International Finance
Tec-City (GIFT IFSC) as a premier global hub for financial technology and innovation. The
proposed framework establishes a robust and sophisticated foundation, demonstrating a
clear understanding of the evolving needs of the FinTech ecosystem.

The core strength of the draft framework lies in its comprehensive, multi-faceted architecture,
which includes distinct sandboxes for regulatory testing, innovation development,
inter-operable solutions, and overseas referrals. This nuanced structure correctly identifies
that financial innovation is not monolithic and requires tailored environments to flourish. It
positions the IFSCA framework favorably against many first-generation sandboxes globally.

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the proposed framework, benchmarking it
against the pioneering and highly successful models in leading jurisdictions, including the
United Kingdom (Financial Conduct Authority - FCA), Singapore (Monetary Authority of
Singapore - MAS), Hong Kong (Hong Kong Monetary Authority - HKMA), and Australia
(Australian Securities and Investments Commission - ASIC).

The analysis reveals that while the framework is fundamentally sound, there are significant
opportunities to enhance its global competitiveness, operational clarity, and consumer
protection mechanisms. The key thematic recommendations presented in this report are
designed to elevate the framework from a strong national initiative to a world-leading
regulatory environment. These themes include:

1. **Enhancing Global Competitiveness:** The global landscape for FinTech innovation is

---

intensely competitive. Leading jurisdictions now offer not just regulatory relief but a
holistic value proposition that includes financial and ecosystem support. To attract the
best global innovators, IFSCA should consider incorporating strategic incentives and
streamlining the applicant journey.
2. **Bolstering Consumer Trust:** For a sandbox to function effectively, it requires the willing
participation of consumers. This participation is built on a foundation of trust. The
framework can be significantly strengthened by introducing more explicit, robust, and
mandatory consumer protection and redressal mechanisms, moving beyond
disclosure-based obligations.
3. **Providing Greater Operational Clarity:** For startups and global firms operating with
limited resources, regulatory ambiguity creates friction, cost, and uncertainty. The
framework can be improved by providing greater upfront clarity on key processes, such
as the scope of available regulatory relaxations and the methodology for setting
boundary conditions.
4. **Refining Key Clauses for Accessibility:** Certain provisions, while well-intentioned, may
inadvertently create barriers to entry for early-stage or foreign FinTechs. Minor
adjustments to clauses related to partnerships and eligibility can make the sandbox more
accessible and inclusive.

This report is structured in two parts. Part A provides a strategic analysis of the framework
and elaborates on the thematic recommendations. Part B presents a detailed,
clause-by-clause feedback table, formatted for direct submission to IFSCA, offering specific,
actionable suggestions grounded in international best practices. The objective of this report
is to provide constructive, evidence-based feedback to assist IFSCA in refining its proposed
framework, ultimately positioning the GIFT IFSC as the preeminent global destination for
financial innovation.

### Section 1: Framework Architecture and Global Positioning

*[Page 2 of 17]*

---

The foundational architecture of the proposed IFSCA framework is one of its most significant strengths. By moving beyond a single, one-size-fits-all model, it demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of the innovation lifecycle. The provision of four distinct environments—the FinTech Regulatory Sandbox (FRS), the FinTech Innovation Sandbox (FIS), the Inter-Operable Regulatory Sandbox (IoRS), and the Overseas Regulatory Referral Mechanism—caters to a wide spectrum of needs, from early-stage ideation in a non-live environment (FIS) to complex, live-market testing of products that span multiple regulatory domains (IoRS).

This multi-faceted approach compares favorably with international peers. The United Kingdom's FCA, which pioneered the concept, established the sandbox as a "safe space" for testing innovative propositions in the market with real consumers. The FCA's model, launched in 2016, primarily focused on solving the problem of regulatory uncertainty for firms whose innovations did not fit neatly within existing rules.⁴ The FCA's FRS aligns directly with this core principle, as articulated in its eligibility criteria, which require a genuine need for testing in a controlled environment and clear benefits to users or the financial ecosystem.

However, the global sandbox landscape has evolved into a competitive arena where jurisdictions vie to attract top-tier talent and capital. Regulatory relief, once a key differentiator, is now a baseline expectation. Leading hubs have recognized that innovators face not only regulatory hurdles but also significant business challenges, such as securing funding and market access. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) exemplifies this evolution. Its framework has matured into "Sandbox Plus," an integrated support system that views the sandbox as an economic development tool as much as a regulatory one. By offering financial grants of up to S$500,000 and providing access to investor matchmaking platforms like "Deal Fridays," MAS directly addresses the commercial pain points of startups, helping them manage cash flow during the pre-revenue testing phase and facilitating their transition to market. This creates a powerful value proposition that extends beyond mere regulatory flexibility. The current IFSCA proposal, while regulatorily robust, is silent on such business-centric incentives. To effectively compete with a hub like Singapore, IFSCA must consider how it can build a similarly supportive ecosystem around its sandbox.

A standout feature of the IFSCA proposal is the formal inclusion of the Inter-Operable Regulatory Sandbox (IoRS) in Part IV, with IFSCA positioned as the Principal Regulator. This is a strategically astute and forward-looking provision. Modern FinTech solutions, particularly in areas like embedded finance, decentralized finance (DeFi), and comprehensive wealth management platforms, often blur the traditional lines between banking, capital markets, and insurance. For an innovator, navigating multiple, siloed regulators for a single product is a significant source of friction, cost, and delay. Jurisdictions like Hong Kong have addressed this by creating linkages between the sandboxes of the HKMA (banking), the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC), and the Insurance Authority (IA), providing a single point of entry for cross-sector trials. The IFSCA framework takes this a step further by establishing a clear "Principal Regulator" model, which promises to streamline coordination and provide a unified interface for applicants. This positions GIFT IFSC as an ideal jurisdiction for testing

sophisticated, next-generation financial products and should be a key element of its international marketing strategy.

Australia's approach offers another point of comparison. The Enhanced Regulatory Sandbox (ERS) administered by ASIC is structured primarily as a licensing exemption framework. It allows eligible firms to test specific services for up to 24 months without needing to obtain a full Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) or Australian Credit Licence (ACL), subject to strict caps on client exposure and mandatory consumer protection conditions. While the IFSCA model is based on a "Limited Use Authorisation" rather than a blanket exemption, the Australian experience underscores the value of providing a longer testing duration for certain types of innovation.

## **Section 2: Enhancing Competitiveness and Attracting Global Innovators**

To establish GIFT IFSC as a top-tier destination for FinTech, the sandbox framework must be not only regulatorily sound but also highly attractive and accessible to the world's most promising innovators. This requires a focus on streamlining the applicant journey and introducing competitive differentiators that address the real-world needs of startups and scale-ups.

The proposed two-stage application process, consisting of a Preliminary and a Final Application, is a logical approach to filter for suitability before requiring a full submission. The commitment to examine the Preliminary Application within thirty working days provides a reasonable degree of timeline certainty. This is comparable to Singapore's MAS, which aims to provide feedback on suitability within 21 working days. A key strength of the proposed IFSCA model is that it appears to be "always open," allowing firms to apply on their own timeline. This is a significant advantage over the cohort-based models that some sandboxes initially adopted and reflects the current best practice, as seen with the FCA's move to an always-open model in 2021 to provide greater flexibility.

*[Page 5 of 17]*

---

However, a critical potential barrier within the approval process is the requirement in Clause 9 for an applicant to have at least one "Testing Partner" as a condition of receiving an "In-principle Approval". While this ensures that a tested solution has a practical path to market, it presents a classic "chicken-and-egg" problem for many desirable applicants, particularly foreign FinTechs or early-stage startups without an established network. An incumbent financial institution may be hesitant to sign a Memorandum of Understanding or Letter of Intent with an unknown entity that has not yet received any formal validation from the regulator. This requirement, if strictly enforced at this stage, could inadvertently function as a gatekeeper, filtering out novel innovators in favor of those who already have strong ties to established players. The FCA's approach is more facilitative; it works with potentially eligible firms to *develop* and *refine* their testing plans, which can include solidifying partnerships during the application process itself.² This transforms the partnership from a rigid prerequisite into a collaborative part of the onboarding journey. IFGCA could significantly enhance the sandbox's accessibility by adopting a similar role, creating a formal mechanism to facilitate introductions between promising applicants and potential testing partners within the GIFT IFSC ecosystem.

Beyond accessibility, competitiveness hinges on the overall value proposition. As demonstrated by Singapore's "Sandbox Plus," a world-class framework should extend beyond regulatory relief. IFSCA has a unique opportunity to build a "Sandbox Plus" or "IFSCA Launchpad" initiative. This could include:

* **Financial Grants:** Introducing a competitive financial grant, modeled on the MAS program, would be a powerful attractor. A grant that co-funds a portion of qualifying expenses (such as manpower and technology costs) would directly alleviate the cash-flow pressures that startups face during the non-revenue-generating testing phase. This would signal that IFSCA is not just a regulator but a vested partner in fostering innovation.
* **Ecosystem and Investor Access:** A formal program to connect sandbox participants with the venture capital, private equity, and corporate venture communities in GIFT IFSC and across India would be invaluable. This could be structured similarly to Singapore's "Deal Fridays" program, which provides a platform for deal-making opportunities and mentorship. Such a program would help successful sandbox graduates secure the funding needed for a full market launch, completing the innovation lifecycle.
* **Expanded Eligibility:** MAS has also expanded its eligibility criteria beyond "first movers" to include "early adopters" of technology. This acknowledges that significant innovation can also come from adapting and improving existing technologies for new markets or use cases. Adopting a similar philosophy would allow IFSCA to foster a broader range of technological adoption and competition within the IFSC.

By integrating these elements, IFSCA can create a holistic support system that addresses both the regulatory and commercial challenges of innovation, making its sandbox a globally compelling proposition.

**Section 3: Calibrating Consumer Protection and Risk Mitigation**

The long-term success and credibility of any regulatory sandbox depend on its ability to foster innovation while robustly protecting consumers and maintaining market integrity. A single high-profile failure that results in significant consumer harm can undermine trust in the entire initiative. The proposed IFSCA framework rightly includes several foundational consumer protection elements. Clause 12 requires FinTech Sandbox Entities (FSEs) to disclose to users that the product is being tested in a sandbox environment, and both Clause 12 and Clause 20 mandate that FSEs obtain written acknowledgment from users that they understand the potential risks. The framework also requires a clearly defined grievance redressal mechanism as part of the evaluation criteria.

While these are necessary provisions, they are currently high-level and place a significant degree of discretion in the hands of the FSE. The most critical weakness lies in Clause 20, which states that the FSE shall "disclose whether any compensation will be provided for potential losses". This phrasing allows an FSE to simply declare that no compensation is available, effectively shifting the entire risk of failure onto the participating consumer. For a nascent ecosystem seeking to build trust, this is an inadequate safeguard. It creates a risk of reputational damage to the sandbox and to GIFT IFSC itself should a test fail and leave consumers with uncompensated losses.

International best practices demonstrate a clear trend towards more stringent and mandatory consumer protection measures. The Australian framework provides a compelling model. Participants in ASIC's Enhanced Regulatory Sandbox are required to:

1. Be a member of the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA), an independent external dispute resolution (EDR) scheme, providing consumers with a free and impartial forum for redress.
2. Hold adequate professional indemnity (PI) insurance to cover potential claims from retail clients.
3. Establish and maintain adequate arrangements for compensating retail clients for loss or damage.

Similarly, the HKMA's Fintech Supervisory Sandbox (FSS) requires participating banks to have clear customer protection measures in place, explicitly including "compensation of any financial losses suffered by customers". The UK's FCA works closely with firms to ensure "appropriate consumer protection safeguards" are in place before any testing begins.

The rationale behind these more robust frameworks is clear: consumer trust is a critical infrastructure for a sandbox. Without the confidence that they will be protected and have access to fair recourse if something goes wrong, consumers will be hesitant to participate in

*[Page 7 of 17]*

---

live tests. This would starve innovators of the real-world data and feedback they need to
validate their products. By making consumer protection mechanisms optional or
disclosure-based, a framework risks creating a low-trust environment.

To be recognized as a top-tier, safe, and responsible environment for financial innovation,
IFSCA should significantly strengthen these provisions. The following enhancements are
recommended:

* **Mandatory Compensation Arrangements:** The framework should be amended to
require FSEs to establish and maintain adequate arrangements to compensate users for
any direct financial losses incurred due to failures or faults in the product or service
being tested.
* **Professional Indemnity Insurance:** Mandating appropriate PI insurance should be a
standard condition for participation in the Regulatory Sandbox (FRS), particularly for
entities dealing with retail customers.
* **Independent Dispute Resolution:** IFSCA should require FSEs to be members of a
recognized independent dispute resolution body, such as an arbitration or mediation
center within GIFT IFSC, or consider establishing a dedicated ombudsman service for
sandbox-related complaints. This externalizes the grievance process to a credible,
impartial third party, providing a much stronger safeguard than an internal mechanism
designed by the FSE itself.

Implementing these changes would not stifle innovation; rather, it would create the trusted
environment necessary for it to thrive, signaling to both global innovators and consumers that
GIFT IFSC is committed to the highest standards of market integrity.

### **Section 4: Providing Operational Clarity and Regulatory Certainty**

For innovators, particularly startups and international firms, time and resources are finite.
Regulatory uncertainty translates directly into increased legal costs, delayed time-to-market,
and difficulty in securing investment. A key attribute of a successful sandbox framework is its
ability to provide clarity and predictability to applicants. While the IFSCA proposal is
comprehensive, several areas could benefit from greater operational clarity.

The most significant area of ambiguity is Clause 16, concerning "Regulatory
Relaxations/Exemptions". The framework adopts a fully bespoke and reactive approach,
stating that relaxations "may be considered" and that the onus is on the applicant to "specify
the regulatory relaxations/ exemptions sought." This approach, while flexible, creates
considerable uncertainty. An applicant, especially one unfamiliar with the nuances of the full
IFSCA regulatory stack, may not know what is permissible to ask for. They might request too
little, rendering the sandbox test unviable, or too much, leading to a protracted negotiation or

*[Page 8 of 17]*

---

outright rejection. This lack of upfront guidance can make the application process daunting
and inefficient.

Leading regulators have developed mechanisms to address this challenge and provide
greater certainty. The UK's FCA, for example, proactively offers a clear menu of "sandbox
tools" that firms can request. These include specific instruments like "restricted
authorisation," "waivers," "no enforcement action letters," "individual guidance," and "informal
steers". This provides applicants with a clear vocabulary and a defined set of options,
effectively turning an open-ended question ("What relaxation do you want?") into a more
structured one ("Which of these tools do you need to conduct your test?").

Similarly, while the MAS sandbox is also bespoke, its guidelines provide valuable upfront
direction by indicating the types of regulations that may be considered for relaxation (e.g.,
relating to capital, management experience, or track record) and, crucially, those that will not
be relaxed (e.g., confidentiality of customer information, AML/CFT compliance, and handling
of customer assets). This manages applicant expectations and focuses the dialogue on
achievable outcomes. Australia's ERS offers the highest degree of certainty by being a
pre-defined licensing exemption with clear, legislated boundaries on eligible products, client
exposure limits (e.g., A5 million aggregate), and a fixed duration of 24 months.

To enhance regulatory certainty, IFSCA should consider publishing a "Guidance Note" to
accompany the main framework. This document would not need to remove the bespoke
nature of the sandbox but could provide:

* An indicative, non-exhaustive list of the types of regulatory provisions that IFSCA may
  consider relaxing for different categories of FinTech activities.
* Clear guidance on regulatory areas that are considered non-negotiable, such as core
  AML/CFT obligations and data privacy laws.
* A framework or methodology for how "Boundary Conditions" (e.g., limits on customers,
  transaction values) will be determined, linking them to the FSE's risk assessment and
  mitigation strategies.

Providing this level of detail would not bind the Authority's discretion but would significantly
demystify the application process. It would empower applicants to submit more targeted and
realistic proposals, reduce the time and cost associated with application preparation, and
signal a mature, transparent, and collaborative regulatory approach. This clarity is a form of
currency for innovators and would be a powerful tool in attracting them to the GIFT IFSC.

## Conclusion

The proposed IFSCA FinTech Sandbox Framework is a well-conceived and ambitious document that provides a strong foundation for fostering a world-class FinTech ecosystem in GIFT IFSC. Its multi-faceted structure, particularly the inclusion of an Inter-Operable Regulatory Sandbox, demonstrates a forward-looking approach that is well-aligned with the complex, cross-sector nature of modern financial innovation.

The analysis and recommendations provided in this report are intended to be constructive, aiming to build upon this strong foundation. By adopting the proposed enhancements, IFSCA can elevate the framework from a solid national initiative to a globally competitive destination for the world's leading FinTech innovators.

The key recommendations can be synthesized into four strategic imperatives:

*[Page 16 of 17]*

---

1. **Compete Holistically:** Move beyond offering only regulatory relief and embrace a broader role as an ecosystem enabler. By introducing financial grants and structured investor access programs, IFSCA can create a holistic value proposition that rivals leading hubs like Singapore.
2. **Prioritize Trust:** Make consumer protection robust and non-negotiable. Mandating compensation arrangements, professional indemnity insurance, and access to independent dispute resolution will build the consumer trust that is essential for the sandbox's long-term success and credibility.
3. **Deliver Certainty:** Reduce ambiguity for applicants by providing greater upfront clarity. Publishing guidance on potential regulatory relaxations and the methodology for setting boundary conditions will demystify the process, reduce friction, and make the sandbox more attractive to innovators.
4. **Ensure Accessibility:** Refine procedural requirements, such as the timing of the "Testing Partner" mandate, to ensure the sandbox is accessible to the most innovative firms, regardless of their size, origin, or existing network.

The implementation of these recommendations will send a powerful signal to the global FinTech community: that GIFT IFSC is not merely open for business, but is actively building the world's most supportive, transparent, and trusted environment for pioneering the future of finance. This will be a critical step in achieving the Authority's objective to develop and regulate a vibrant and globally significant financial services center.
