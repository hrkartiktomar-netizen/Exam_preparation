## English Language

**Direction (1-5): Read the passage carefully then answer the questions given below.**
For decades, scientists have tried to determine whether Mars once had conditions suitable for life. While the planet today is cold, dry, and exposed to intense radiation, geological evidence suggests that early Mars may have been warmer and wetter. One key factor influencing planetary habitability is the presence of a thick atmosphere, which can retain heat and protect the surface from harmful solar winds. Understanding when Mars lost its atmosphere remains a central question in planetary science.
A recent study conducted by researchers at Stanford University revisits this issue using new atmospheric loss models. Published in *Nature Astronomy*, the study argues that Mars may have retained a significant portion of its atmosphere until about 3.6 billion years ago— later than earlier theories that placed atmospheric collapse at around 4.2 billion years ago. This extended atmospheric lifespan could have allowed liquid water to persist on the surface for a longer duration.
The research was led by Dr. Michael Anders, who used computer simulations to model how solar wind interacted with Mars's atmosphere over time. Because Mars lacks a strong global magnetic field today, solar particles are able to strip atmospheric gases directly from the planet. However, the team's simulations suggest that Mars once possessed localized magnetic regions strong enough to slow this atmospheric erosion.
To support their model, the researchers analyzed data from NASA's MAVEN mission, which measures how charged particles escape from Mars's upper atmosphere. The data showed variability in atmospheric loss rates that could be explained by intermittent magnetic shielding in Mars's early history. This finding challenges the assumption that atmospheric loss occurred rapidly and uniformly across the planet.
If Mars retained its atmosphere longer than previously believed, it strengthens the case for sustained surface water and potentially microbial life. Anders emphasizes that planetary environments evolve gradually, and small protective mechanisms—such as partial magnetic shielding—can have large long-term effects. The study highlights the importance of revising long-standing assumptions as new data and modeling techniques become available.
