An Analysis and Recommendations
Report on the Proposed IFSCA FinTech
Sandbox Framework: A Roadmap to
Global Leadership

Executive Summary

The International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) has taken a commendable and
proactive step in proposing a dedicated and unied FinTech Sandbox Framework. This
initiative is a critical enabler for realizing the vision of the Gujarat International Finance
Tec-City (GIFT IFSC) as a premier global hub for nancial technology and innovation. The
proposed framework establishes a robust and sophisticated foundation, demonstrating a
clear understanding of the evolving needs of the FinTech ecosystem.

The core strength of the dra framework lies in its comprehensive, multi-faceted architecture,
which includes distinct sandboxes for regulatory testing, innovation development,
inter-operable solutions, and overseas referrals. This nuanced structure correctly identies
that nancial innovation is not monolithic and requires tailored environments to ourish. It
positions the IFSCA framework favorably against many rst-generation sandboxes globally.

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the proposed framework, benchmarking it
against the pioneering and highly successful models in leading jurisdictions, including the
United Kingdom (Financial Conduct Authority - FCA), Singapore (Monetary Authority of
Singapore - MAS), Hong Kong (Hong Kong Monetary Authority - HKMA), and Australia
(Australian Securities and Investments Commission - ASIC).

The analysis reveals that while the framework is fundamentally sound, there are signicant
opportunities to enhance its global competitiveness, operational clarity, and consumer
protection mechanisms. The key thematic recommendations presented in this report are
designed to elevate the framework from a strong national initiative to a world-leading
regulatory environment. These themes include:

 1. Enhancing Global Competitiveness: The global landscape for FinTech innovation is
      intensely competitive. Leading jurisdictions now oer not just regulatory relief but a
      holistic value proposition that includes nancial and ecosystem support. To aract the
      best global innovators, IFSCA should consider incorporating strategic incentives and
      streamlining the applicant journey.
 2. Bolstering Consumer Trust: For a sandbox to function eectively, it requires the willing
      participation of consumers. This participation is built on a foundation of trust. The
      framework can be signicantly strengthened by introducing more explicit, robust, and
      mandatory consumer protection and redressal mechanisms, moving beyond
      disclosure-based obligations.
 3. Providing Greater Operational Clarity: For startups and global rms operating with
      limited resources, regulatory ambiguity creates friction, cost, and uncertainty. The
      framework can be improved by providing greater upfront clarity on key processes, such
      as the scope of available regulatory relaxations and the methodology for seing
      boundary conditions.
 4. Rening Key Clauses for Accessibility: Certain provisions, while well-intentioned, may
      inadvertently create barriers to entry for early-stage or foreign FinTechs. Minor
      adjustments to clauses related to partnerships and eligibility can make the sandbox more
      accessible and inclusive.

This report is structured in two parts. Part A provides a strategic analysis of the framework
and elaborates on the thematic recommendations. Part B presents a detailed,
clause-by-clause feedback table, formaed for direct submission to IFSCA, oering specic,
actionable suggestions grounded in international best practices. The objective of this report
is to provide constructive, evidence-based feedback to assist IFSCA in rening its proposed
framework, ultimately positioning the GIFT IFSC as the preeminent global destination for
nancial innovation.

Part A: Strategic Analysis and Thematic
Recommendations

This section provides a holistic assessment of the proposed IFSCA FinTech Sandbox
Framework, contextualizing it within the global regulatory landscape and identifying
overarching opportunities for enhancement.

Section 1: Framework Architecture and Global Positioning
The foundational architecture of the proposed IFSCA framework is one of its most signicant
strengths. By moving beyond a single, one-size-ts-all model, it demonstrates a sophisticated
understanding of the innovation lifecycle. The provision of four distinct environments—the
FinTech Regulatory Sandbox (FRS), the FinTech Innovation Sandbox (FIS), the Inter-Operable
Regulatory Sandbox (IoRS), and the Overseas Regulatory Referral Mechanism—caters to a
wide spectrum of needs, from early-stage ideation in a non-live environment (FIS) to complex,
live-market testing of products that span multiple regulatory domains (IoRS).

This multi-faceted approach compares favorably with international peers. The United
Kingdom's FCA, which pioneered the concept, established the sandbox as a "safe space" for
testing innovative propositions in the market with real consumers. The FCA's model, launched
in 2016, primarily focused on solving the problem of regulatory uncertainty for rms whose
innovations did not t neatly within existing rules.4 The IFSCA's FRS aligns directly with this
core principle, as articulated in its eligibility criteria, which require a genuine need for testing
in a controlled environment and clear benets to users or the nancial ecosystem.

However, the global sandbox landscape has evolved into a competitive arena where
jurisdictions vie to aract top-tier talent and capital. Regulatory relief, once a key
dierentiator, is now a baseline expectation. Leading hubs have recognized that innovators
face not only regulatory hurdles but also signicant business challenges, such as securing
funding and market access. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) exemplies this
evolution. Its framework has matured into "Sandbox Plus," an integrated support system that
views the sandbox as an economic development tool as much as a regulatory one. By oering
nancial grants of up to S$500,000 and providing access to investor matchmaking plaorms
like "Deal Fridays," MAS directly addresses the commercial pain points of startups, helping
them manage cash ow during the pre-revenue testing phase and facilitating their transition
to market. This creates a powerful value proposition that extends beyond mere regulatory
exibility. The current IFSCA proposal, while regulatorily robust, is silent on such
business-centric incentives. To eectively compete with a hub like Singapore, IFSCA must
consider how it can build a similarly supportive ecosystem around its sandbox.

A standout feature of the IFSCA proposal is the formal inclusion of the Inter-Operable
Regulatory Sandbox (IoRS) in Part IV, with IFSCA positioned as the Principal Regulator. This is
a strategically astute and forward-looking provision. Modern FinTech solutions, particularly in
areas like embedded nance, decentralized nance (DeFi), and comprehensive wealth
management plaorms, oen blur the traditional lines between banking, capital markets, and
insurance. For an innovator, navigating multiple, siloed regulators for a single product is a
signicant source of friction, cost, and delay. Jurisdictions like Hong Kong have addressed
this by creating linkages between the sandboxes of the HKMA (banking), the Securities and
Futures Commission (SFC), and the Insurance Authority (IA), providing a single point of entry
for cross-sector trials. The IFSCA framework takes this a step further by establishing a clear
"Principal Regulator" model, which promises to streamline coordination and provide a unied
interface for applicants. This positions GIFT IFSC as an ideal jurisdiction for testing
sophisticated, next-generation nancial products and should be a key element of its
international marketing strategy.

Australia's approach oers another point of comparison. The Enhanced Regulatory Sandbox
(ERS) administered by ASIC is structured primarily as a licensing exemption framework. It
allows eligible rms to test specic services for up to 24 months without needing to obtain a
full Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) or Australian Credit Licence (ACL), subject to
strict caps on client exposure and mandatory consumer protection conditions. While the
IFSCA model is based on a "Limited Use Authorisation" rather than a blanket exemption, the
Australian experience underscores the value of providing a longer testing duration for certain
types of innovation.

Section 2: Enhancing Competitiveness and Aracting Global
Innovators

To establish GIFT IFSC as a top-tier destination for FinTech, the sandbox framework must be
not only regulatorily sound but also highly aractive and accessible to the world's most
promising innovators. This requires a focus on streamlining the applicant journey and
introducing competitive dierentiators that address the real-world needs of startups and
scale-ups.

The proposed two-stage application process, consisting of a Preliminary and a Final
Application, is a logical approach to lter for suitability before requiring a full submission. The
commitment to examine the Preliminary Application within thirty working days provides a
reasonable degree of timeline certainty. This is comparable to Singapore's MAS, which aims to
provide feedback on suitability within 21 working days. A key strength of the proposed IFSCA
model is that it appears to be "always open," allowing rms to apply on their own timeline.
This is a signicant advantage over the cohort-based models that some sandboxes initially
adopted and reects the current best practice, as seen with the FCA's move to an
always-open model in 2021 to provide greater exibility.
However, a critical potential barrier within the approval process is the requirement in Clause 9
for an applicant to have at least one "Testing Partner" as a condition of receiving an
"In-principle Approval". While this ensures that a tested solution has a practical path to
market, it presents a classic "chicken-and-egg" problem for many desirable applicants,
particularly foreign FinTechs or early-stage startups without an established network. An
incumbent nancial institution may be hesitant to sign a Memorandum of Understanding or
Leer of Intent with an unknown entity that has not yet received any formal validation from
the regulator. This requirement, if strictly enforced at this stage, could inadvertently function
as a gatekeeper, ltering out novel innovators in favor of those who already have strong ties to
established players. The FCA's approach is more facilitative; it works with potentially eligible
rms to develop and rene their testing plans, which can include solidifying partnerships
during the application process itself.2 This transforms the partnership from a rigid prerequisite
into a collaborative part of the onboarding journey. IFSCA could signicantly enhance the
sandbox's accessibility by adopting a similar role, creating a formal mechanism to facilitate
introductions between promising applicants and potential testing partners within the GIFT
IFSC ecosystem.

Beyond accessibility, competitiveness hinges on the overall value proposition. As
demonstrated by Singapore's "Sandbox Plus," a world-class framework should extend beyond
regulatory relief. IFSCA has a unique opportunity to build a "Sandbox Plus" or "IFSCA
Launchpad" initiative. This could include:

 ● Financial Grants: Introducing a competitive nancial grant, modeled on the MAS
      program, would be a powerful aractor. A grant that co-funds a portion of qualifying
      expenses (such as manpower and technology costs) would directly alleviate the
      cash-ow pressures that startups face during the non-revenue-generating testing
      phase. This would signal that IFSCA is not just a regulator but a vested partner in
      fostering innovation.

 ● Ecosystem and Investor Access: A formal program to connect sandbox participants
      with the venture capital, private equity, and corporate venture communities in GIFT IFSC
      and across India would be invaluable. This could be structured similarly to Singapore's
      "Deal Fridays" program, which provides a plaorm for deal-making opportunities and
      mentorship. Such a program would help successful sandbox graduates secure the
      funding needed for a full market launch, completing the innovation lifecycle.

 ● Expanded Eligibility: MAS has also expanded its eligibility criteria beyond "rst movers"
      to include "early adopters" of technology. This acknowledges that signicant innovation
      can also come from adapting and improving existing technologies for new markets or use
      cases. Adopting a similar philosophy would allow IFSCA to foster a broader range of
      technological adoption and competition within the IFSC.

By integrating these elements, IFSCA can create a holistic support system that addresses
both the regulatory and commercial challenges of innovation, making its sandbox a globally
compelling proposition.
Section 3: Calibrating Consumer Protection and Risk Mitigation

The long-term success and credibility of any regulatory sandbox depend on its ability to foster
innovation while robustly protecting consumers and maintaining market integrity. A single
high-prole failure that results in signicant consumer harm can undermine trust in the entire
initiative. The proposed IFSCA framework rightly includes several foundational consumer
protection elements. Clause 12 requires FinTech Sandbox Entities (FSEs) to disclose to users
that the product is being tested in a sandbox environment, and both Clause 12 and Clause 20
mandate that FSEs obtain wrien acknowledgment from users that they understand the
potential risks. The framework also requires a clearly dened grievance redressal mechanism
as part of the evaluation criteria.

While these are necessary provisions, they are currently high-level and place a signicant
degree of discretion in the hands of the FSE. The most critical weakness lies in Clause 20,
which states that the FSE shall "disclose whether any compensation will be provided for
potential losses". This phrasing allows an FSE to simply declare that no compensation is
available, eectively shiing the entire risk of failure onto the participating consumer. For a
nascent ecosystem seeking to build trust, this is an inadequate safeguard. It creates a risk of
reputational damage to the sandbox and to GIFT IFSC itself should a test fail and leave
consumers with uncompensated losses.

International best practices demonstrate a clear trend towards more stringent and mandatory
consumer protection measures. The Australian framework provides a compelling model.
Participants in ASIC's Enhanced Regulatory Sandbox are required to:

 1. Be a member of the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA), an independent
      external dispute resolution (EDR) scheme, providing consumers with a free and impartial
      forum for redress.

 2. Hold adequate professional indemnity (PI) insurance to cover potential claims from retail
      clients.

 3. Establish and maintain adequate arrangements for compensating retail clients for loss or
      damage.

Similarly, the HKMA's Fintech Supervisory Sandbox (FSS) requires participating banks to have
clear customer protection measures in place, explicitly including "compensation of any
nancial losses suered by customers". The UK's FCA works closely with rms to ensure
"appropriate consumer protection safeguards" are in place before any testing begins.

The rationale behind these more robust frameworks is clear: consumer trust is a critical
infrastructure for a sandbox. Without the condence that they will be protected and have
access to fair recourse if something goes wrong, consumers will be hesitant to participate in
live tests. This would starve innovators of the real-world data and feedback they need to
validate their products. By making consumer protection mechanisms optional or
disclosure-based, a framework risks creating a low-trust environment.

To be recognized as a top-tier, safe, and responsible environment for nancial innovation,
IFSCA should signicantly strengthen these provisions. The following enhancements are
recommended:

 ● Mandatory Compensation Arrangements: The framework should be amended to
      require FSEs to establish and maintain adequate arrangements to compensate users for
      any direct nancial losses incurred due to failures or faults in the product or service
      being tested.

 ● Professional Indemnity Insurance: Mandating appropriate PI insurance should be a
      standard condition for participation in the Regulatory Sandbox (FRS), particularly for
      entities dealing with retail customers.

 ● Independent Dispute Resolution: IFSCA should require FSEs to be members of a
      recognized independent dispute resolution body, such as an arbitration or mediation
      center within GIFT IFSC, or consider establishing a dedicated ombudsman service for
      sandbox-related complaints. This externalizes the grievance process to a credible,
      impartial third party, providing a much stronger safeguard than an internal mechanism
      designed by the FSE itself.

Implementing these changes would not stie innovation; rather, it would create the trusted
environment necessary for it to thrive, signaling to both global innovators and consumers that
GIFT IFSC is commied to the highest standards of market integrity.

Section 4: Providing Operational Clarity and Regulatory Certainty

For innovators, particularly startups and international rms, time and resources are nite.
Regulatory uncertainty translates directly into increased legal costs, delayed time-to-market,
and diculty in securing investment. A key aribute of a successful sandbox framework is its
ability to provide clarity and predictability to applicants. While the IFSCA proposal is
comprehensive, several areas could benet from greater operational clarity.

The most signicant area of ambiguity is Clause 16, concerning "Regulatory
Relaxations/Exemptions". The framework adopts a fully bespoke and reactive approach,
stating that relaxations "may be considered" and that the onus is on the applicant to "specify
the regulatory relaxations/ exemptions sought." This approach, while exible, creates
considerable uncertainty. An applicant, especially one unfamiliar with the nuances of the full
IFSCA regulatory stack, may not know what is permissible to ask for. They might request too
lile, rendering the sandbox test unviable, or too much, leading to a protracted negotiation or
outright rejection. This lack of upfront guidance can make the application process daunting
and inecient.

Leading regulators have developed mechanisms to address this challenge and provide
greater certainty. The UK's FCA, for example, proactively oers a clear menu of "sandbox
tools" that rms can request. These include specic instruments like "restricted
authorisation," "waivers," "no enforcement action leers," "individual guidance," and "informal
steers". This provides applicants with a clear vocabulary and a dened set of options,
eectively turning an open-ended question ("What relaxation do you want?") into a more
structured one ("Which of these tools do you need to conduct your test?").

Similarly, while the MAS sandbox is also bespoke, its guidelines provide valuable upfront
direction by indicating the types of regulations that may be considered for relaxation (e.g.,
relating to capital, management experience, or track record) and, crucially, those that will not
be relaxed (e.g., condentiality of customer information, AML/CFT compliance, and handling
of customer assets). This manages applicant expectations and focuses the dialogue on
achievable outcomes. Australia's ERS oers the highest degree of certainty by being a
pre-dened licensing exemption with clear, legislated boundaries on eligible products, client
exposure limits (e.g., A5 million aggregate), and a xed duration of 24 months.

To enhance regulatory certainty, IFSCA should consider publishing a "Guidance Note" to
accompany the main framework. This document would not need to remove the bespoke
nature of the sandbox but could provide:

 ● An indicative, non-exhaustive list of the types of regulatory provisions that IFSCA may
      consider relaxing for dierent categories of FinTech activities.

 ● Clear guidance on regulatory areas that are considered non-negotiable, such as core
      AML/CFT obligations and data privacy laws.

 ● A framework or methodology for how "Boundary Conditions" (e.g., limits on customers,
      transaction values) will be determined, linking them to the FSE's risk assessment and
      mitigation strategies.

Providing this level of detail would not bind the Authority's discretion but would signicantly
demystify the application process. It would empower applicants to submit more targeted and
realistic proposals, reduce the time and cost associated with application preparation, and
signal a mature, transparent, and collaborative regulatory approach. This clarity is a form of
currency for innovators and would be a powerful tool in aracting them to the GIFT IFSC.

Conclusion

The proposed IFSCA FinTech Sandbox Framework is a well-conceived and ambitious
document that provides a strong foundation for fostering a world-class FinTech ecosystem in
GIFT IFSC. Its multi-faceted structure, particularly the inclusion of an Inter-Operable
Regulatory Sandbox, demonstrates a forward-looking approach that is well-aligned with the
complex, cross-sector nature of modern nancial innovation.

The analysis and recommendations provided in this report are intended to be constructive,
aiming to build upon this strong foundation. By adopting the proposed enhancements, IFSCA
can elevate the framework from a solid national initiative to a globally competitive destination
for the world's leading FinTech innovators.

The key recommendations can be synthesized into four strategic imperatives:
 1. Compete Holistically: Move beyond oering only regulatory relief and embrace a
      broader role as an ecosystem enabler. By introducing nancial grants and structured
      investor access programs, IFSCA can create a holistic value proposition that rivals
      leading hubs like Singapore.

 2. Prioritize Trust: Make consumer protection robust and non-negotiable. Mandating
      compensation arrangements, professional indemnity insurance, and access to
      independent dispute resolution will build the consumer trust that is essential for the
      sandbox's long-term success and credibility.

 3. Deliver Certainty: Reduce ambiguity for applicants by providing greater upfront clarity.
      Publishing guidance on potential regulatory relaxations and the methodology for seing
      boundary conditions will demystify the process, reduce friction, and make the sandbox
      more aractive to innovators.

 4. Ensure Accessibility: Rene procedural requirements, such as the timing of the "Testing
      Partner" mandate, to ensure the sandbox is accessible to the most innovative rms,
      regardless of their size, origin, or existing network.

The implementation of these recommendations will send a powerful signal to the global
FinTech community: that GIFT IFSC is not merely open for business, but is actively building the
world's most supportive, transparent, and trusted environment for pioneering the future of
nance. This will be a critical step in achieving the Authority's objective to develop and
regulate a vibrant and globally signicant nancial services center.
